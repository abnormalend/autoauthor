## Framing

- genre_noun — "romance novel"
- pillar_noun — "central relationship"
- comps — Courtney Milan, Talia Hibbert, Emily Henry, Alyssa Cole, Kennedy Ryan, Georgette Heyer
- seed_persona — a romance novelist who has written across the genre's full range — contemporary, historical, queer, closed-door and explicit — and who never proposes a couple whose only reason for being together is that the plot put them in the same building
- reader_persona — a romance reader who finishes 60+ books a year, knows every trope by name and loves them when they are built properly, and stops trusting a book the moment the obstacle turns out to be something one honest sentence would fix
- writer_persona — a working romance author and RWA-circuit workshop teacher who reads for beat placement, for whether both arcs actually close, and for the difference between chemistry and proximity

## Pillar Dimensions

Where this pack is the primary, the relationship is the plot. Not a thread
running beside the plot — the spine of it. Everything below is scored on
the assumption that if you removed the relationship, there would be no book
left, and that the external plot exists to apply pressure to the
relationship rather than the other way round.

Where this pack is loaded as a SECONDARY, that assumption does not hold and
must not be applied. The book's main plot belongs to the primary pack, and
scoring it as though it existed to serve the romance would penalise a book
for being what it is. Read every dimension below as scoping to the book's
romantic thread: judge that thread by these standards, at whatever scale
the book gives it, and do not require it to be the spine.

### Romancing the Beat

`beat_system` is `romancing-the-beat`, not Save the Cat. Its four phases
and their beats, with approximate percentage marks, are the structure the
outline is judged against:

  1. **Setup** (0-10%) — Introduce both leads in their separate lives, each
     with a goal that is not the other person. Then the No Way / Adhesion:
     the two meet and something forces them into continued contact neither
     can walk away from.
  2. **Falling in Love** (10-50%) — Inkling of Desire, Deepening Desire,
     Maybe This Could Work, and the Midpoint of Love: the first moment the
     relationship is real to both of them, and the moment the stakes of
     losing it become concrete.
  3. **Retreating from Love** (50-75%) — Inkling of Doubt, Deepening Doubt,
     Retreating, Shields Up. The barrier reasserts itself, and it must
     reassert itself for reasons the first half planted.
  4. **Fighting for Love** (75-100%) — the Break Up / Black Moment, the Dark
     Night of the Soul in which each lead confronts what they actually
     believe, the Grand Gesture in which one or both pay a real cost, and
     the resolution: HEA or HFN.

An outline that puts the black moment at 90% and the reconciliation two
pages later has not written a Dark Night of the Soul; it has written a
delay. An outline whose leads are separated for most of Act II has written
two novellas.

### Scored dimensions

- attraction_specificity [cap 6] — Why THESE two? Excellent looks like each lead noticing things about the other that no third party in the book would notice, rooted in what each of them specifically lacks, fears, or admires. A gap looks like attraction asserted rather than caused — "there was something about him" — or attraction that is really just proximity plus availability. Test: swap one lead for a different character of the same age, gender, and social position, keeping the setting and plot identical. If the attraction still reads as plausible, the book has written chemistry between roles rather than between people; score 6 max. Second test: name three specific things each lead notices about the other. If the documents cannot supply three for BOTH leads, score 6 max.
- barrier_integrity [cap 6] — What keeps them apart must be structural: competing obligations, incompatible futures, a cost one of them genuinely cannot pay, a loyalty that is not negotiable. Excellent looks like a barrier that both leads can see clearly and still cannot dissolve. A gap looks like a misunderstanding, a withheld fact, or a secret whose only defense is that nobody asked. Test: write the single conversation that would end the barrier. If that conversation exists and either lead could plausibly have it by the quarter mark without changing who they are, score 6 max. Note that a barrier which is structural in Act I but which the plot quietly removes by external event — the job ends, the fiancé leaves, the family relents — has failed the same test late; score 6 max for that too.
- relationship_progression [cap 6] — The relationship must have a different state at the end of each scene the leads share than it had at the start. Excellent looks like an escalation ladder where each rung is earned and none is skipped: what they now know about each other, what they now risk, what they can no longer say casually. A gap looks like scenes of pleasant company that advance nothing, or a jump from wary to intimate with the intervening rungs summarized. Test: walk the outline and mark every chapter in which the two are together. If three or more consecutive such chapters leave the relationship in the state they found it, score 6 max. Also check the beats above land near their marks — a Midpoint of Love after 60% or a Break Up before 60% is a structural miss, not a stylistic choice.
- black_moment_cost [cap 6] — The break must be caused by the barrier the book has been building, and it must cost something the reader watched them earn. Excellent looks like each lead making a defensible choice that is nonetheless the one that loses them the other person. A gap looks like a break caused by an overheard half-sentence, a lie of omission with no motive behind it, or a third party's manufactured interference. Test: name what is actually lost at the black moment, and name who chose it. If the answer to the second is "neither of them" or "a misunderstanding," score 6 max. If the black moment is resolved by the same information that caused it simply being stated, score 6 max.
- mutual_transformation [cap 6] — Both leads change, and each is the specific cause of the other's change. Excellent looks like two wound/want/need/lie chains where each lead's need is exactly the thing the other person's existence makes undeniable. A gap looks like one lead arriving finished and functioning as the cure for the other — the fixer and the fixed. Test: for EACH lead, name the belief they hold in chapter 1 that they cannot hold at the end, and name the scene in which the other lead makes it untenable. If you can do this for only one of the two, score 6 max. A lead whose only arc is "learns to trust again," with no scene where that trust is specifically tested and specifically costly, does not count as an arc.

## Genre Contract

These bind the central romantic relationship. When this pack is loaded as a
secondary, they bind the book's romantic thread, not its main plot.

- The central relationship resolves HEA (happily ever after) or HFN (happy for now): both leads alive, together, and choosing each other on the page at the end. This is not a stylistic preference. Romance readers treat a central relationship that ends in separation, death, or ambiguity as a broken promise rather than an artistic choice — a book that wants that ending is a love story, and it should not use this pack.
- The resolution comes from a decision one or both leads make, not from an external event that removes the barrier for them.
- Both leads are present for the resolution. It does not happen off-page, in summary, or in an epilogue that skips the reconciliation itself.
- Neither lead's consent is treated as an obstacle to be overcome. Coercion, deception into intimacy, and persistence past refusal are not courtship in this genre.

## World Sections

Romance has almost no world in the sense other genres mean it. What it has
instead is a pressure system: the specific arrangement of jobs, families,
obligations, money, and physical space that forces these two people into
contact and then makes staying together expensive. Every section below must
produce something that CONSTRAINS a choice somewhere in the outline. A
detail that constrains nothing is decoration and counts against the score,
not for it. Keep this document short and load-bearing — a fifty-page world
bible in a romance is usually a sign the relationship is underbuilt.

- Proximity & Adhesion
- Work, Money, Obligation
- Family & Community
- The Barrier's Machinery
- Private Space
- Internal Consistency Rules

### Proximity & Adhesion
The mechanism that keeps these two in each other's path after the meeting,
and specifically why neither can simply stop showing up. Name the thing
that would have to break for them to lose contact entirely — a shared job,
a lease, a custody arrangement, a small town with one road out, a shared
obligation to a third person. Then name what it costs each of them to
maintain that contact, because a proximity with no cost generates no
tension.

### Work, Money, Obligation
What each lead does for money, what they are paid, who depends on that
money, and what they lose if it stops. Romance stakes are almost never
death; they are a lease, a licence, a promotion, a child's stability, a
reputation in a field with a long memory. Name at least one obligation per
lead that has a claim on them older than the relationship does, because
that is the claim the barrier will be built from.

### Family & Community
The people with standing to have an opinion: parents, siblings, ex-partners,
in-laws, employers, congregations, group chats, the neighbours. Who would
be told, who would find out anyway, and what each of them can actually do
about it — withdraw money, withdraw childcare, withdraw a job, withdraw
belonging. At least two people whose disapproval carries a concrete
consequence, not just a bad feeling.

### The Barrier's Machinery
The single most important section in this document. State the barrier as a
mechanism, not a mood: what specifically prevents these two from being
together, who or what enforces it, and what it would cost each of them to
break. Then write the conversation that would dissolve it, and state
plainly why neither lead can have that conversation without ceasing to be
who they are. If you cannot write that paragraph, the barrier is a
misunderstanding and the book does not have a plot yet.

### Private Space
Where these two can be unobserved, and where they cannot. Romance runs on
access: a kitchen at 2am, a car, a supply closet, a shared wall, a house
with a mother in it. Name the specific places intimacy of any register is
possible here and the specific reasons the rest of the map forecloses it,
along with the sensory signature of each — a scene set in one must be
impossible to relocate to another without loss.

### Internal Consistency Rules
Hard constraints a writer must not violate: travel times between the two
leads' worlds, who can afford what, who works which shift, who has custody
which weeks, who would notice an absence and when. This genre has no magic,
which means the things that break it are coincidence, sudden money, a
convenient business trip, and a phone that stops working exactly when the
plot needs it to. Write down the ones this book must not use.

## Cast Requirements

1. **Lead A** — derived from the seed.
   - Full wound/want/need/lie chain
   - Three sliders with justification
   - Arc type (positive/negative/flat)
   - Detailed speech pattern (8 dimensions, with example lines)
   - Physical habits and tells tied to their work, class, and wound
   - At least 2 secrets
   - A goal that exists independently of the other lead
   - Key relationships mapped

2. **Lead B** — the same depth as Lead A, with no shortcuts. This is the
   genre's most common structural failure: a fully realized protagonist
   opposite a love interest who is a set of attractive attributes. Lead B
   needs their own wound/want/need/lie chain, their own independent goal,
   and their own arc that closes. If the registry is visibly thinner for
   one of the two, the book has a leading role and a prize, not a couple.

3. **The person or body that benefits from them staying apart** — not a
   villain. Someone whose legitimate interests are served by the barrier
   holding: a parent protecting a family, an employer enforcing a policy, a
   community with a rule, an ex with a claim. Full wound/want/need/lie
   chain.

4. **One confidant per lead** — the person each talks to, who is allowed to
   say the thing the lead cannot say to themselves. These two must not be
   interchangeable; they should want different things for their respective
   leads.

5. **At least one further character** the story needs — someone whose life
   the relationship materially changes: a child, a business partner, a
   sibling, a dependent.

## Drafting Rules

25. Every scene the two leads share must leave the relationship in a different state than it started — more known, more risked, more foreclosed. A scene of pleasant company that changes nothing is a scene to cut, however well it reads.
26. Attraction is shown through specific noticing, never asserted. What does this character see that no one else in the room would see, and what does it cost them to have noticed it?
27. Interiority is the plot here. The reader must know, in every scene, what each POV lead believes about where they stand — and the gap between what the two believe is where the tension lives.
28. Banned phrases, on top of the base list: "butterflies in her stomach", "electricity/sparks between them", "the world fell away", "she'd never felt this way before", "he was everything she never knew she needed", "her breath hitched", "a heat pooled low in her belly", "safe for the first time in her life".

## Seed Prompt

Persona (adopt while generating):

You are a romance novelist who has written across the genre's full
range — contemporary and historical, queer and straight, closed-door
and explicit, comic and devastating. You generate novel concepts that
are SPECIFIC, SURPRISING, and STRUCTURALLY SOUND. Every concept names
two people who have a reason to want each other that no one else
would have, and a reason they cannot simply have each other that a
single honest conversation would not dissolve. You never propose a
couple whose only connection is that the plot put them in the same
building.

Required concept fields (these romance fields and phrasings replace
the neutral scaffold's versions of the same fields):

WORLD: The pressure system this relationship lives inside — the work,
  the family, the town, the institution, the money. Not a backdrop:
  the specific arrangement that forces these two into contact and
  then makes staying together expensive. Make it SENSORY and make it
  material: what each of them is paid, who depends on them.
COUPLE/BARRIER: Who the two leads are, what specifically draws each
  to the other (something no third party in the book would notice —
  not looks, not proximity), and the STRUCTURAL barrier between them.
  The barrier must be a mechanism, not a misunderstanding: competing
  obligations, incompatible futures, a loyalty that is not
  negotiable, a cost one of them genuinely cannot pay. Name what it
  would cost each of them to break it — that cost is the black
  moment.
ARCS: For EACH lead, the belief they hold at the start that they
  cannot hold at the end, and why the other person specifically is
  the one who makes it untenable. Two arcs, both closing. Neither
  lead may arrive already finished.
TENSION: What's the central conflict? It must be both PERSONAL (what
  each lead stands to lose in themselves) and LARGER (it implicates a
  family, a workplace, a community, a child). These two must be in
  tension with each other.
THEME: What question does this story explore? Not a message — a
  genuine question with no easy answer.
WHY IT'S NOT GENERIC: One sentence on what makes this different from
  the standard romance premise.

Aim for DIVERSITY across the ten concepts:
  - Span heat registers — at least one that clearly wants to be
    closed-door and at least one that clearly wants to be explicit;
    this pack constrains neither
  - Span the subgenres this pack serves: contemporary, historical,
    small-town, workplace, second-chance, friends-to-lovers. Do not
    propose a concept whose centre of gravity is a dedicated pack —
    romantic suspense, dark romance, paranormal romance, and romantasy
    each ship their own, and a premise that wants one of those wants
    that pack rather than this one
  - At least one queer pairing and at least one where neither lead is
    white or Anglo-American
  - Span life stages — not ten couples in their late twenties; at
    least one over fifty, at least one with children already
  - At least one where the two leads want incompatible GOOD things,
    not where one of them is wrong
  - At least one comic and at least one that hurts
  - Vary who holds the power in the pairing, and vary which of the
    two has more to lose
  - Mix of tones: warm, wry, tender, angsty, melancholy

DO NOT generate:
  - The Big Misunderstanding — any premise whose obstacle is a
    withheld fact, an overheard half-sentence, or a secret with no
    motive behind keeping it
  - A trope with its machinery removed: fake dating where either lead
    could walk away at no cost, marriage of convenience with no
    consequence for annulment, forced proximity with an unlocked door
  - Rescue as romance — one lead's entire arc being saved, fixed, or
    financially rescued by the other
  - A love triangle as the central engine
  - A lead with no goal, job, or life outside the relationship
  - Enemies-to-lovers where the enmity is bickering rather than
    genuinely opposed interests
  - The reformed abuser, the stalker recast as devoted, or coercion
    written as intensity
  - A third-act breakup manufactured by a jealous third party
  - Billionaires whose money solves the plot
