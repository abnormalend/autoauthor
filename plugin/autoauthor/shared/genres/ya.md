Young adult is an age category, not a genre. YA fantasy, YA contemporary,
YA thriller, YA historical and YA romance are all real and all different
from each other, which is exactly why this is a modifier: it changes who
the book is for and how it is told, and leaves the primary pack owning what
kind of book it is.

What it constrains is narrower than the reputation suggests. YA is not
sanitized fiction; it is fiction whose protagonist is a teenager and whose
narration takes that seriously. The category's genuine promises are about
whose story it is, not about what may be depicted. The Hunger Games is YA
and the violence in it is graphic; this pack therefore says nothing about
violence.

## Framing

This modifier deliberately supplies no `genre_noun` and no `pillar_noun`.
The primary pack owns both — a YA fantasy is still a fantasy novel with a
magic system. Where a rubric names the genre, read the primary's
`genre_noun` and add the category: "a fantasy novel for young adults".
The persona and comps values below do override the primary's, because the
panel personas must be reading as this audience's advocates.

- comps — Elizabeth Acevedo, Angie Thomas, Nina LaCour, Leigh Bardugo, Adam Silvera, E. Lockhart, Courtney Summers
- seed_persona — a YA novelist who writes the teenager's own version of the story rather than an adult's memory of being one, and who never proposes a premise whose crisis an available, competent adult would simply resolve
- reader_persona — a seventeen-year-old who reads three or four books a month, who detects being lectured to within a page and stops reading, and who can tell instantly when a book's teenagers were written from the outside
- writer_persona — a YA editor and school-visit veteran who reads for whether the protagonist causes the ending, for whether the interiority is a teenager's or a nostalgic adult's, and for the exact sentence where a book stops trusting its reader

## Genre Contract

These bind the book as a whole when this pack is loaded, alongside every
promise the primary pack makes.

- The protagonist is a teenager — roughly 14 to 18 — and the book is narrated from inside that perspective. Not a child, not a young adult in their twenties, and not an adult remembering.
- The decisive choices belong to the teenager. An adult may help, obstruct, or arrive too late, but the protagonist's own action causes the climax and its outcome. A book in which competent adults could have solved the problem, and are merely kept off the page to prevent it, has breached this.
- The book does not moralize. The ending may be hard, sad, hopeful, or unresolved, but it does not resolve into a stated lesson, and no character delivers the book's meaning aloud to the protagonist in the final chapter.
- The teenage characters are treated as full people with real interior lives, not as the not-yet-finished versions of adults. Their concerns are rendered at the scale they have to the people having them.
- The declared `heat` and `language` levels are honoured in both directions. `heat: warm` means attraction and physical intimacy are on the page and are not rendered explicitly; consummation, where it happens, is not depicted in the detail an adult register would use. Where another loaded pack clamps an axis BELOW this pack's level, that clamp governs and this promise yields to it — a YA inspirational novel is bound at `closed-door`, and closing the door earlier is not a breach of this promise. What the promise forbids in the other direction is an explicit register, at any resolved level.

## Drafting Rules

25. Write in immediate present-tense attention, whatever the grammatical tense. The narration stays at the distance of what the protagonist notices right now, not the distance of someone who has already survived it. Retrospective framing — "I would learn later", "it was the last time I ever" — reads as an adult narrator holding the reader's hand, and belongs to a different category.
26. Interiority runs hot and specific. A teenage POV thinks in absolutes, revises them mid-paragraph, and is more embarrassed than afraid. Give the reader the thought as it arrives, including the ones the character would be mortified to have on record — the unfairness of it, the exact person they want to prove wrong. slop_score.py's telling check will flag some of these declaratives ("She was furious."); at this register that is expected and small — absorb the penalty rather than rewriting around the regex, which produces the hedged interiority the rule forbids.
27. Never write down to the reader. Condescension shows up as: explaining a reference the character would not need explained, having an adult character voice the book's judgment, resolving a moral question in the narration rather than in a scene, and softening a consequence at the last moment because the reader is young. Any of these is a rewrite, not a polish.
28. Adults are people with their own reasons, not obstacles or furniture. A parent, teacher, or guardian who is absent, oblivious, or conveniently unhelpful must be so for a reason the book has established — and never so that the plot can survive.
29. The stakes are the ones a seventeen-year-old actually has: belonging, reputation, a place to live, a future that is still negotiable, the first time an adult is wrong in a way that cannot be un-seen. Write these at full weight. A book that treats them as small has already lost the reader it was written for.
30. Dialogue sounds like these specific teenagers, not like Teen Dialogue. Current slang dates a book within eighteen months and reads as costume; get the rhythm right instead — the deflection, the incompleteness, the joke made instead of the sentence that was hard to say.
31. Honour the declared `heat` register in both directions. Attraction, wanting, and physical intimacy belong on the page in full; the explicit rendering of sex does not. Write the wanting, not around it.
