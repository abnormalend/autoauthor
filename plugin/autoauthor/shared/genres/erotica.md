- seed_persona — an erotica novelist who has published across the range from literary to commercial, who starts from what two specific people want that they cannot admit to wanting, and who never proposes a premise whose sex scenes could be reordered without loss
- reader_persona — an erotica reader who reads dozens of these a year, who can tell within two pages whether the writer has ever been in a body, and who puts a book down the moment a scene repeats a scene they have already read in it

## Pillar Dimensions

In this genre the erotic journey IS the plot. Not a thread beside it, not a
reward for plot progress — the spine. Everything below is scored on the
assumption that if you removed the erotic content there would be no book
left, and that whatever external plot exists is there to apply pressure to
desire rather than the other way round. A book where the sex could be cut
and the story would still stand is a novel with sex in it; that book should
load this pack as a modifier over its real primary, not as its primary.

Three distinctions are worth making before scoring, because most of the
genre's failures are one of them collapsing.

**Erotic want and emotional want are not the same want.** What a character
wants done to them and what they want it to mean come apart, and the gap
between the two is where this genre finds its plot. Erotica that flattens
is almost always erotica in which the two are identical — where being
desired and being loved are one undifferentiated hunger, and so nothing can
develop, only repeat.

**Escalation is not intensity.** A book that raises the volume every scene
has not escalated; it has got louder. Escalation is the changing of what is
at stake — what is now known, permitted, refused, owed, or impossible to
take back.

**Explicitness is not specificity.** The register this pack declares is
explicit, which settles what may be named and nothing else. Naming an act
plainly is the floor. The dimension `embodiment` scores what is built on
top of it, and a book can be entirely explicit and entirely generic.

Read `encounter_ledger.md` (this pack's artifact — see `## Artifacts`)
alongside the outline. It is the primary evidence for `escalation_variation`
and `erotic_consequence`, both of which are claims about the relationship
between scenes, and neither of which can be checked by reading any one
scene. A missing or unfilled ledger is a real gap in the plan.

### Scored dimensions

- desire_architecture [cap 6] — Each principal must want two distinguishable things: something physical, and something the physical thing is supposed to mean to them. Excellent looks like a book that can state both for every principal in different words, where the gap between them is what the encounters keep failing to close, and where at least one principal is wrong about their own second answer. A gap looks like desire as undifferentiated appetite — everyone wants everyone, and the only variable is opportunity. Test: for each principal write one sentence naming what they want done, and one naming what they want it to mean. If for any principal the two sentences say the same thing twice, score 6 max. Second test: swap one principal for a different character of the same age and social position, keeping the setting and encounters identical. If the encounters still read as plausible, the book has written appetite between roles rather than desire between people; score 6 max.
- escalation_variation [cap 6] — The encounters must form a sequence, and each must differ from the ones before it along a named axis: who is present, who initiates, what register it is in, what is refused, where it happens, what it risks, what is said aloud. Excellent looks like a ledger whose `What is new` column is filled with a different answer every row, and an arc of anticipation, deferral, and consummation whose deferrals are load-bearing rather than delaying. A gap is scene-to-scene sameness — the genre's signature failure and the acute form of the freshness decay the base drafting rules fight after chapter 6. Test: read the ledger's `What is new` column top to bottom. If any two consecutive encounters differ only in location and duration, score 6 max. If three or more rows repeat the same anticipation-to-consummation shape without a deferral or an interruption between them, score 6 max. Also check the front: if the first full consummation lands before the 20% mark and nothing is withheld after it, the book has spent its escalation and must run on repetition; score 6 max.
- consent_and_power [cap 6] — This is craft, not compliance. The reader must be able to tell, from the text, that each participant wants what is happening, and where power is unequal the text must know it is. Excellent looks like willingness that is legible in specific behaviour — who initiates, who slows down, what is asked and answered — plus at least one moment where someone states a limit and it is honoured on the page, and, where the book is about power exchange, terms established before the scene that uses them. A gap looks like willingness assumed by the author and never shown, or a scene whose tension comes from not knowing whether one participant agrees. Test: for each encounter, name the specific line, gesture, or initiation that makes each participant's willingness legible. If any encounter has none for any participant, score 6 max. If the book depicts power exchange and the ledger shows no scene in which its terms are set, or no scene in which a limit is invoked and respected, score 6 max. Note that outright coercion presented as seduction is a Genre Contract breach, which the rubric handles separately; do not double-count it here.
- embodiment [cap 6] — Physical specificity and interiority, at once, sustained. This is where show-don't-tell is most demanding, because the two easy failures pull opposite ways: choreography with nobody inside it, and feeling with no body attached. Excellent looks like sensation reported through a particular nervous system — this character's history, injuries, embarrassments, appetites, and vocabulary — with the interior line running unbroken through the scene rather than resuming afterwards. A gap looks like anatomical stage directions, or a paragraph of abstract rapture that could be about anything. Test: take any 300 words of erotic prose and count the details that only this character, in this body, in this room, could supply. If fewer than three, score 6 max. Second test: check whether the POV character's thinking continues through the encounter. If the interiority stops at the start of the scene and resumes after it, score 6 max.
- erotic_consequence [cap 6] — Every encounter must change something that cannot be changed back. Excellent looks like a ladder in which each encounter alters what the participants know about each other, what they can no longer say casually, what they now owe, or what they can no longer have — so that the encounters cannot be reordered. A gap looks like a set of interchangeable set pieces between which the characters reset to their starting positions. Test: for each row of the ledger, name what is true afterwards that was not true before, in terms other than "they had sex". If three or more rows have no answer, score 6 max. Second test: try moving the final encounter to the first position. If nothing in the book breaks, the sequence has no arc; score 6 max.

## Genre Contract

These bind the book's erotic content. When this pack is loaded as a
modifier, they bind the erotic content of whatever the primary's plot is,
not the plot itself.

- The declared heat level is delivered, in both directions. This pack declares `explicit`, so the encounters central to the book are rendered on the page in full. A book that promises explicit and fades to black at the moment of consummation has breached this exactly as a closed-door book that delivers explicit would have. Where a resolved `content_register` clamps heat below `explicit`, that clamp governs the book and this pack is the wrong one to have loaded.
- Every participant in every erotic scene is an adult, and is established as an adult on the page before their first such scene. This is not satisfied by the absence of a stated age.
- Consent is legible in the text rather than assumed by the author. For each encounter, a reader can point to what makes each participant's willingness clear — initiation, a stated yes, an asked and answered question, behaviour no reader would misread.
- Coercion, incapacitation, and deception into sex are never written as seduction. Where a book depicts them, it depicts them as what they are and never as the erotic payoff the reader is invited to enjoy.
- The book has more than one erotic scene, and the encounters are dramatized in scene rather than summarized. An erotic journey narrated in retrospect is not this genre.

## World Sections

The world of an erotic novel is a system of access, privacy, exposure, and
cost. Every section below must produce something that constrains a choice
somewhere in the outline — a room that can be reached only at certain
hours, a person who would notice, a body that has a limit. A detail that
constrains nothing is decoration and counts against the score. Keep this
document short and load-bearing; a long world bible here is usually a sign
the desire is underbuilt.

- Access & Privacy
- Bodies, Limits & Practicalities
- Power & Where It Actually Comes From
- Exposure & Its Cost
- The Vocabulary of This World
- Internal Consistency Rules

### Access & Privacy
Where these people can be unobserved, and where they cannot. Erotica runs
on access: who holds which key, which door locks, who is home when, which
wall is thin, what the hours of the building are, who has a car. Name the
specific places an encounter is possible and the specific reasons the rest
of the map forecloses it, with a sensory signature for each — a scene set
in one must be impossible to relocate to another without loss. Then name
what it costs to get to each of them, because access with no cost generates
no anticipation.

### Bodies, Limits & Practicalities
The physical facts the book must not forget: age, health, stamina, injury,
pain, contraception, disease, cycles, medication, disability, what hurts
the next day. Write them down for each principal. This section is what
keeps the encounters happening to bodies rather than to avatars, and it is
also where the honest obstacles live — an erotic obstacle that comes from a
body is always better than one that comes from a misunderstanding.

### Power & Where It Actually Comes From
Who can end this, and at what cost to whom. Money, employment, immigration
status, housing, custody, reputation, seniority, physical strength, the
capacity to walk away. Erotica is unusually interested in power and
unusually often careless about it: state which asymmetries are real in this
world, which ones the characters have negotiated and on what terms, and
which the book intends to eroticize. An asymmetry the book plays with but
has not written down is the one that will read as coercion.

### Exposure & Its Cost
Who would find out, how, and what they could actually do about it — end an
employment, end a marriage, end a tenancy, end a standing in a community.
Name at least two people whose knowledge carries a concrete consequence
rather than a bad feeling. This is where most of the genre's real stakes
come from, because the stakes of erotica are almost never death.

### The Vocabulary of This World
The specific register this book's bodies are described in, decided here
rather than improvised per scene: clinical, vernacular, archaic, tender,
crude, or a documented mixture that shifts by POV. Name the words this book
uses and the words it refuses. Doing this once in the world bible is what
stops the drafter reaching for the euphemism register the pack's banned
list forbids, and what keeps two POV characters from sounding identical
inside a scene.

### Internal Consistency Rules
Hard constraints a writer must not violate: who is where and when, who has
a key, who is capable of what, how long anything takes, who would be
missed. This genre has no magic; the things that break it are a door that
is unlocked because the scene needs it, an interruption that never comes, a
body with no refractory period, and a household that empties on cue. Write
down the ones this book must not use.

## Cast Requirements

1. **Principal A** — derived from the seed.
   - Full wound/want/need/lie chain
   - Erotic want and emotional want, stated separately, in different
     words. This is scored under `desire_architecture`, so "to be
     wanted" is not sufficient for either.
   - Three sliders with justification
   - Arc type (positive/negative/flat)
   - Detailed speech pattern (8 dimensions, with example lines),
     including how their register changes in an encounter — most people
     do not speak the same way
   - Physical habits, tells, and the body's own history: what they are
     self-conscious about, what they have never done, what they will not
     do and why
   - At least 2 secrets

2. **Principal B** — the same depth, with no shortcuts. The genre's most
   common structural failure is a fully realized protagonist opposite a
   partner who is a set of attractive attributes and a willingness. B
   needs their own two wants, their own limits, and their own arc that
   closes. If the registry is visibly thinner for one of the two, the
   book has a protagonist and a device.

3. **Any further participant** who appears in an encounter — depth
   proportional to page time, but never zero. Anyone the book puts in a
   scene needs a want of their own and a reason to be there that is not
   the author's arrangement.

4. **The person or body whose knowledge would cost something** — an
   employer, a spouse, a family, a congregation, a licensing board, a
   flatmate. Not a villain: someone whose interest in knowing is
   legitimate. Full wound/want/need/lie chain if they appear on the page.

5. **One confidant** — the person a principal can say the true thing to,
   who is allowed to name what the principal is avoiding. Not
   interchangeable with the other principal.

## Plot Architecture

KEY PLOT ARCHITECTURE (adapt the exact chapter numbers to the seed; these
are proportional guideposts across an 18-24 chapter novel). The beats are
Save the Cat's, placed at these marks and read against the erotic arc
rather than an external plot.

- **Act I** (roughly the first quarter): establish each principal
  separately — what they want done, what they want it to mean, and the
  distance between those. Establish the world's access rules and what
  privacy costs here. Catalyst: something makes contact between them
  unavoidable rather than optional. Anticipation, not consummation, is
  the business of this act; what happens physically may be small, and
  should already be specific.
- **Act II Part 1** (roughly 25-50%): escalation with deferral. Each
  encounter must add something the ledger can name, and at least one
  must be interrupted, refused, or deliberately postponed by a character
  rather than by circumstance. Midpoint: the first encounter after which
  neither can pretend this is only appetite — the point where the
  emotional want becomes visible to at least one of them, and the stakes
  of it become concrete.
- **Act II Part 2** (roughly 50-77%): consequence. What the encounters
  have made true starts to cost something in the world — exposure,
  obligation, jealousy, an asymmetry that was tolerable and is not now.
  The gap between the two wants stops being interesting and starts being
  a problem. All Is Lost: a principal gets exactly what they said they
  wanted and finds it does not deliver what they actually wanted.
- **Act III** (roughly 77-100%): the principals must decide what to want.
  The final encounter is the book's argument and must be impossible
  earlier — it should depend on something established after the
  midpoint. The resolution shows the aftermath of the choice, and need
  not be a happy ending; this pack, unlike romance, makes no promise
  about how the relationship ends.

CONSTRAINTS:
- No encounter may be interchangeable with another. If two could swap
  chapters without adjustment, one of them is not doing work.
- Deferral must come from a character's decision at least as often as
  from circumstance. Interruption by plot is a device; refusal by a
  person is a scene.

## Artifacts

### encounter_ledger.md

The encounter ledger is this genre's accounting for the one claim that
cannot be checked by reading any single scene: that the encounters form a
sequence rather than a set. Sameness is invisible from inside a scene —
each one can be well written and the book still be repetitive — so
`escalation_variation` and `erotic_consequence` need the scenes laid side
by side. Nothing else in the project does that: `canon.md` records facts,
and the foreshadowing ledger in `outline.md` tracks plants and payoffs, but
neither records what a scene added or what it made impossible afterwards.

**Format.** A single markdown table with these seven columns, one row per
encounter, in chapter order:

| # | Chapter | Who | Wants (erotic / emotional) | Register & initiation | What is new | What it changes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ch_04 | Mira, Jonah | to be touched deliberately / to be chosen over the work | wordless, she initiates | first deliberate touch; nothing said | they can no longer pretend the proximity is accidental |
| 2 | ch_07 | Mira, Jonah | to finish what ch_04 started / to keep it deniable | urgent, he initiates, she sets the hour | first full encounter; she names a limit and leaves | the limit exists now and can be broken later |
| 3 | ch_09 | Mira, Jonah | to be given instructions / to be trusted with them | negotiated aloud, terms set | first time either says what they want; a word that ends it | there is an agreement, so there is something to breach |
| 4 | ch_12 | Mira, Jonah | — deferred — | interrupted; Devi in the corridor | first refusal that costs them | exposure stops being hypothetical |

Column by column:

- **#** — sequence number, so a reordering during revision is visible.
- **Chapter** — the chapter in the project's `ch_NN` form. An encounter
  spanning two chapters is one row, at its first chapter.
- **Who** — every participant present, including anyone who witnesses or
  interrupts. A person in the room is in this column.
- **Wants (erotic / emotional)** — the two wants for this encounter,
  separated by a slash, for the POV principal at minimum and preferably
  for both. These change across the book; that change is the arc
  `desire_architecture` is scored on. If the two halves ever read as the
  same statement, that is the finding.
- **Register & initiation** — the mode of the scene and who moved first:
  wordless or negotiated, tender or urgent, public risk or private
  safety, who asked and who answered. Two rows with the same entry here
  are two rows the reader will experience as one scene.
- **What is new** — the single thing this encounter contains that no
  earlier one did. `Nothing` is never a valid entry; a row that cannot
  fill this column is a scene to cut or to rewrite. A deferral, refusal,
  or interruption is a legitimate entry and often the strongest one.
- **What it changes** — what is true afterwards that was not true
  before, stated in terms other than "they had sex". Knowledge, debt,
  permission, foreclosure, risk. This column is what
  `erotic_consequence` is scored against.

Below the table, add one line stating the total number of encounters, the
chapter of the first full consummation as a percentage of the book, and
whether any two consecutive rows share a `Register & initiation` entry.
Stating those explicitly stops each reader recomputing them differently.

**Which phase fills it.** `seed` creates the file from this template
when it scaffolds the project. `foundation` fills it against the
completed outline, after `characters.md` exists — the `Wants` column cannot
be written before the principals have their two wants — and re-checks it
whenever the outline or either principal's want chain changes.
`draft` adds a row whenever a chapter contains an encounter the
ledger does not have, and corrects `Register & initiation` and `What is
new` when a chapter lands differently from the plan. `revise` and
`review` re-verify the whole table against the manuscript, and are
the passes most likely to find that two rows have quietly converged.

**What the rubric checks.** The judge reads this file alongside
`outline.md` and confirms:

  - Every row has a non-empty, specific `What is new` and `What it
    changes`.
  - No two consecutive rows share a `Register & initiation` entry.
  - The `Wants` column changes across the book for at least one
    principal.
  - At least one row is a deferral, refusal, or interruption caused by a
    character rather than by circumstance.
  - The final row could not have been the first — something in it depends
    on an earlier row.
  - Every participant listed in `Who` appears in `characters.md`.

An absent or unfilled ledger is scored as a gap in
`escalation_variation` and `erotic_consequence` alike, because those two
dimensions have no other evidence to read.

Note for the modifier slot: `resolve_genre.py` deliberately drops a
modifier's `artifacts` from the merged config, so a project loading this
pack as a modifier will not have an encounter ledger. That is correct —
artifacts belong to the primary genre. Where this pack is a modifier, the
drafting rule below that refers to the ledger applies only if the project
has one.

## Drafting Rules

25. Write the encounters in full, at the declared register. The heat level in the resolved `content_register` is a floor as well as a ceiling: where it is `explicit`, do not cut away, summarize, or resolve the scene in white space. Where a modifier has clamped it lower, that clamp governs and this pack should not have been loaded.
26. Name bodies and acts in the register `world.md` established under "The Vocabulary of This World". Decide the register once, in the world bible, and hold it — the euphemism is not modesty, it is imprecision, and it reads as embarrassment on the writer's part.
27. Interiority does not pause for the encounter. The POV character keeps thinking, noticing, misreading, and wanting throughout; a scene where the interior line stops and the choreography starts is the single most reliable tell of unfelt prose in this genre.
28. Every encounter must contain something no earlier encounter contained, and you must be able to say what it is in one clause before you begin drafting it. Record it in `encounter_ledger.md` as you write, where the project has one. If you cannot name it, the scene is a repeat and belongs in summary or out of the book.
29. Willingness is shown, never assumed. Someone initiates, someone answers, someone changes the pace and is heeded. At least once in the book, a limit is stated and honoured on the page — the scene where someone is heard is what makes every other scene readable as desire rather than as pressure.
30. Sensation is reported through a particular body with a particular history. Metaphors come from the POV character's trade, injuries, appetites, and vocabulary — not from weather, not from the sea, and not from fire.
31. Vary the shape of the scenes as well as their content. Not every encounter runs approach, escalation, consummation, aftermath in that order; some are interrupted, some begin in the middle, some are remembered afterwards from the wrong side, and at least one should end without consummation.

## Seed Prompt

Persona (adopt while generating):

You are an erotica novelist who has published across the genre's full
range — literary and commercial, contemporary and historical, queer and
straight, tender and severe. You generate novel concepts that are
SPECIFIC, SURPRISING, and STRUCTURALLY SOUND. Every concept you propose
names two people who want different things from the same act, and an
arrangement of privacy, power, and cost that makes each encounter
expensive to have. You never propose a premise whose encounters could be
reordered without loss, and you never propose one where desire is simply
appetite plus opportunity.

Required concept fields (these erotica fields and phrasings replace the
neutral scaffold's versions of the same fields):

WORLD: The system of access, privacy, and exposure this book runs on —
  the building, the institution, the household, the season, the job.
  Not a backdrop: the specific arrangement that makes privacy scarce
  and being found out expensive. Make it SENSORY, and make it
  constraining: who holds a key, who is home when, which wall is thin.
DESIRE/GAP: For EACH principal, what they want done and what they want
  it to mean — in different words. Name the gap between those two, and
  say which principal is wrong about their own second answer. If the
  two answers say the same thing twice, the concept is not ready.
ESCALATION: The shape of the sequence — roughly four to six encounters,
  each named by the ONE thing it contains that the previous ones did
  not, and at least one deferral or refusal that a character chooses
  rather than circumstance imposing. Say what the last encounter
  depends on that makes it impossible earlier.
POWER & TERMS: What is unequal between these people in the world's own
  terms — money, employment, standing, immigration status, strength —
  and how the book intends to handle it: negotiated aloud, quietly
  reversed in private, or examined. Name the moment the terms are set
  and the moment a limit is invoked.
TENSION: What's the central conflict? It must be both PERSONAL (what
  each principal stands to lose in themselves) and LARGER (it
  implicates a job, a household, a marriage, a community). These two
  must be in tension with each other.
THEME: What question does this story explore? Not a message — a genuine
  question with no easy answer.
WHY IT'S NOT GENERIC: One sentence on what makes this different from
  the standard erotica premise.

Aim for DIVERSITY across the ten concepts:
  - Vary who holds power and vary whether the book reverses it
  - At least one queer pairing, at least one where neither principal is
    white or Anglo-American, at least one with more than two principals
  - Span life stages — at least one principal over fifty, at least one
    with a body that has been through something
  - Vary the register: tender, severe, comic, melancholy, clinical
  - At least one historical and at least one where the constraint is
    technological or institutional rather than social
  - At least one that ends together and at least one that does not;
    this pack makes no promise about the ending
  - Vary the source of deferral — a job, a body, a household, a rule
    the principals made themselves
  - At least one where the two principals want incompatible good
    things, not where one of them is wrong

DO NOT generate:
  - Desire as appetite plus opportunity — two people who want each
    other for no reason a third party could not have supplied
  - A premise whose encounters could be reordered without adjustment
  - The billionaire with a contract and a playroom, and any variant
    where wealth removes every obstacle
  - Coercion, incapacitation, or deception recast as seduction or
    intensity; the reformed abuser; the pursuit that continues past a
    refusal and is rewarded
  - Any premise requiring a participant who is not an adult, or whose
    age the concept leaves unstated
  - The stranger with no name, no history, and no consequence, as the
    book's whole engine
  - A partner who exists as a set of attractive attributes and a
    willingness
  - Sex as the reward for plot progress — a thriller or a romance with
    encounters bolted on at intervals
  - Virginity as the stakes, or a first time that resolves a character's
    entire arc
