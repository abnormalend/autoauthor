Cozy is a tone, not a genre. It is applied most often to mystery, and it is
not limited to it: cozy fantasy, cozy speculative fiction, and small-town
cozy contemporary are all real shelves. What is constant across them is a
contract about scale and safety — the reader is promised that the world of
this book will still be habitable at the end, and the tension comes from
that promise being genuinely at risk rather than from the threat of gore.

Cozy is not the absence of seriousness. The commonest failure of the tone
is a book so committed to comfort that nothing in it can go wrong, which
leaves the reader nothing to worry about and nothing to be relieved by. The
warmth is the thing being threatened, not a substitute for a threat.

## Framing

This modifier supplies no `genre_noun` and no `pillar_noun` — the primary
pack owns both, and a cozy mystery is still a mystery novel with a puzzle.
Where a rubric names the genre, read the primary's `genre_noun` with this
tone applied: "a cozy mystery novel". The comps and personas below do
override the primary's, because the panel must read as this tone's
audience.

- comps — Alexander McCall Smith, Louise Penny, Richard Osman, Jenny Colgan, Travis Baldree, M.C. Beaton, Barbara Pym
- seed_persona — a novelist of small communities who builds a place first and a plot second, who knows that a cozy premise fails when nothing in it can actually be lost, and who never proposes a village that is only charming
- reader_persona — a cozy reader who has read hundreds of these, who is there for the place and the people and would follow them into a book with no plot at all, and who will not forgive a book that treats a death as set dressing
- writer_persona — an editor of the tone who reads for whether the community is a real social body or a set of quirks, for whether the food and the weather and the routine are doing structural work or filling space, and for the exact point at which warmth becomes sentimentality

## Genre Contract

These bind the book as a whole when this pack is loaded, alongside every
promise the primary pack makes.

- Where the book contains violence — and a cozy mystery necessarily does — it happens off the page. The reader learns of it by aftermath, report, or discovery, never by being present for it. This covers the death, any assault, and the climax: a confrontation may be tense and frightening, and the harm in it is not depicted.
- The community survives the story. The place and the people who make it are still there at the end, changed and still habitable. A cozy that ends with the village emptied, the shop closed for good, or the ensemble dispersed has broken its central promise.
- The protagonist's competence comes from their ordinary life, not from credentials. A baker, a bookseller, a vicar, a retired teacher: what they know how to do is what their actual life taught them, and the book must show that knowledge being the thing that works. Where the book gives them professional standing instead, it is a different tone.
- No cruelty is dwelt on and no character is degraded for the reader's entertainment. Unpleasant people exist and are held to account; the book does not invite the reader to enjoy their humiliation.
- The declared `language` level is honoured. Profanity, where it appears, stays mild; the tone does not run on it.

## Drafting Rules

25. Warmth is built from specifics, never asserted. The reader must feel the place is worth protecting because of what is in it — the particular bell over the particular door, the customer who always asks for the same thing — not because the narration says the village is lovely. A sentence that tells the reader how to feel about a place is a sentence to replace with the detail that would have produced the feeling.
26. Place, food, weather, and routine must do structural work. They mark time, they reveal character, they carry information, and they are where the reader learns what is normal here — which is the only way an interruption of normal can land. A meal described because cozy books have meals in them is filler; a meal in which someone eats nothing, or brings the wrong thing, is a scene.
27. The body still has to matter. Someone is dead, or something real has been lost, and the book must not treat it as a puzzle piece. At least one character grieves in a way the narrative respects, and the protagonist's discovery of the aftermath is written for its weight, not hurried past to get back to the tea. This is the discipline the tone lives or dies by.
28. Warmth is not sentimentality. Sentimentality is emotion the book has not earned and asks for anyway: the tearful reconciliation with no cost, the neighbour whose kindness has no inconvenience attached, the ending that forgives everyone. Every act of kindness in this book should cost the person performing it something, and the reader should be able to name what.
29. The ensemble is a social body, not a set of quirks. Each recurring character wants something the others' wants complicate, and knows something about someone else that they choose not to say. A character defined by one repeated trait — the gossip, the grump, the eccentric aunt — needs a second thing that contradicts the first before they appear a third time.
30. Violence and its aftermath reach the reader through consequence and discovery. Write the found room, the changed routine, the phone that goes unanswered, the person who cannot go back into that building. The absence of the scene is not a gap to apologize for; handled well it is more frightening than the scene would have been.
31. Comedy comes from character and from manners, not from cruelty. The joke is never at the expense of someone's poverty, appearance, disability, or grief, and the funniest people in the book should be the ones the book likes most.
