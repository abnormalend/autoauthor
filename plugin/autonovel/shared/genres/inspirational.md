Inspirational is an axis, not a genre. An inspirational romance is a romance,
an inspirational mystery is a mystery, and the plot machinery of neither
changes. What the category adds is a content boundary the shelf actually
enforces and a third arc running alongside the plot arc and the character
arc: a movement in the protagonist's relationship to faith. That is all this
pack governs; the primary owns everything else.

This pack is a faith-arc discipline rather than a statement of doctrine, and
names Christian fiction — 69,000 titles on romance.io, plus a substantial
Amish shelf — as its dominant instance rather than its definition. The reason
is practical: the rules below concern causation, cost, and dramatization, and
they hold identically for a Mennonite widow, a lapsed Catholic detective, or
an observant Jewish protagonist. A pack encoding a theology would produce a
judge scoring a book's tradition instead of its construction. Nothing here
licenses devotional writing either — a book that is moving because it means
well is this category's characteristic failure, not its aim.

## Framing

This modifier supplies no `genre_noun` and no `pillar_noun` — the primary
owns both, and an inspirational mystery is still a mystery novel with a
puzzle. Where a rubric names the genre, read the primary's `genre_noun` with
this axis applied: "an inspirational romance novel". The comps and personas
below do override the primary's, because the panel must read as this
category's own audience rather than the wider genre's.

- comps — Francine Rivers, Beverly Lewis, Dee Henderson, Lynn Austin, Karen Kingsbury, Marilynne Robinson, Chaim Potok
- seed_persona — a novelist of faith-facing fiction who builds the spiritual arc and the plot as one mechanism rather than two, and who never proposes a premise that is a conversion scene with a story attached to it
- reader_persona — a reader who buys this category on purpose and has read hundreds of them, who can feel a sermon coming a chapter away and resents it, and who is far more offended by a cheap conversion than by a hard question the book leaves open
- writer_persona — an editor at a house that publishes this category, who reads for whether the faith arc causes anything in the plot, for whether belief is ever shown costing a character something, and for the exact page where the book stops trusting the reader to draw the conclusion

## Genre Contract

These bind the book as a whole when this pack is loaded, alongside every
promise the primary pack makes.

- The protagonist's relationship to faith is in a materially different state at the end than at the start, and the change is dramatized rather than asserted. At least one scene shows them acting on belief at a price a reader can name — money, safety, standing, a relationship, something they wanted. A character who states their trust and is never required to act on it at a cost has not been given an arc.
- That arc is causally braided into the primary's plot. At least one decision the protagonist makes out of the changed relationship to faith alters what happens in the main plot — the investigation, the relationship, the quest, whatever the primary owns. A spiritual turn that merely occurs near the resolution, and would leave the plot unchanged if deleted, is a breach.
- Neither arc resolves the other. The primary's central conflict closes on its own terms and is never discharged by a conversion or a profession of faith: where the primary is romance, the black moment is not resolved by one lead coming to believe; where it is mystery, the culprit is not identified by revelation. Both arcs must be able to close, and neither may substitute for the other.
- No character exists in order to be corrected. Every character who does not share the protagonist's faith has a want, a competence, and a function in the plot that is independent of their belief, and none is converted merely to finish their business in the book.
- Difficulty is met, not removed. Where a hardship the book has established resolves, the protagonist has paid something toward it on the page; a rescue that arrives to spare them a cost the book set up is a breach. Doubt, grief, and losses that stay lost are permitted and expected — a faith the book never tests has nothing to dramatize.
- The declared `heat`, `violence`, and `language` levels are honoured as ceilings on the whole book. They bound it without promising the content occurs: an inspirational mystery may contain no romance and an inspirational romance no violence, and both satisfy this. Consummation, where it happens, is off the page; violence is depicted where the plot needs it and never dwelt on; profanity does not appear, including in softened stand-ins.

## Drafting Rules

25. Dramatize belief; never assert it. Faith reaches the reader through what a character does when doing it is expensive, not through what they say they hold. A line of dialogue stating a conviction is not the scene — it is the summary of a scene you have not written. Prayer belongs on the page only when its outcome is genuinely uncertain to the person praying.
26. Give unbelief its best argument. The doubting or objecting character gets the sharpest line in the room, and is right about something the book never takes back. Their objection is answered — if at all — by an event, never by narration and never by another character out-talking them.
27. Nobody speaks the book's meaning aloud. No pastor, elder, grandmother, or dying mentor delivers the theme in the last quarter, and the narration does not summarize what a scene has already shown. Where a conclusion has to reach the reader, let them reach it from a character's own bad decision.
28. Scripture, sermon, hymn, and liturgy do scene work or they come out. Write them as they land on one specific character in one specific moment — misheard, resented, half-remembered, arriving at the wrong time — not as authority quoted at the reader. If a passage could be cut without changing what a character does next, cut it.
29. Loss stays lost. The illness does not lift because someone prayed, the debt is not paid by an unexpected cheque, the dead do not turn out to have been better off. Consolation is a thing characters offer each other and often get wrong; it is not a plot mechanism, and every time it repairs something the book had broken, the reader trusts the next hardship less.
30. A congregation is a social body, not a chorus. It contains faction, money, gossip, and people who are there for reasons that have nothing to do with belief. The community's approval must be able to cost the protagonist something, or its withdrawal means nothing when it comes.
31. Closed-door means the door closes, not that desire is absent. Write the wanting in full, up to the threshold, and cut on the choice rather than on a fade. Anger reaches the reader through what a character does with their hands and where they go afterwards — never through asterisks, dashes, or a euphemism straining to be noticed.
32. Banned phrases, on top of the base list: "a peace that passed all understanding", "she felt God's presence", "everything happens for a reason", "God had a plan for her", "put it in God's hands", "her heart swelled", "a still small voice told him", "she had never felt so loved".
